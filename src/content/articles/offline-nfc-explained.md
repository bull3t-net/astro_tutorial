<p class="lead">“NFC” describes the way a card communicates at close range. It doesn’t, on its own, tell you whether the next step needs the internet.</p>

## The difference is in the payload.

A chip can store a website address. Tap it, and a compatible phone opens a profile page. That is convenient, but loading the profile usually depends on connectivity and the service hosting it.

A direct-data card instead stores the contact information itself in a suitable record format. A compatible reader can interpret those details locally, without fetching them from a website.

## Offline doesn’t mean universal.

Phone hardware, operating systems, record types and reader apps all matter. In particular, native background NFC behaviour on iPhones does not treat arbitrary contact records the same way it treats supported web links. Some direct-data workflows may require a compatible reader app.

Ask for a test on the devices your customers actually use. An optional direct-data contact QR offers another reading route, but scanner support still needs to be checked.

## What changes when your details change?

You cannot update a direct-data card by editing a hosted profile. An unlocked, writable chip can be rewritten with suitable tools, within its storage limits. A printed QR needs reprinting. If remote updates are your priority, that trade-off should be part of your decision.

## A simpler relationship.

Cardistry’s direct-data model has no monthly profile subscription. The card is a tool for your next conversation, not a reason to add another recurring service.
